School literacy in a post pandemic world

UX researcher for an EdTech literacy platform, investigating how K-12 teachers discovered, evaluated, and used Newsela platform to support students post-pandemic.

My role

As a UX researcher contractor at BairesDev, I worked with Newsela's UX research team for over a year, contributing to multiple research projects across the platform, focused on literacy.

My work spanned generative and evaluative research, moderating user interviews with K-12 teachers, synthesizing findings, and delivering reports, covering topics such as curriculum alignment, content evaluation, platform user experience, and reading literacy formats. These studies uncovered how teachers and students were leveraging the platform.

All sessions were recruited and conducted through the UserTesting platform. I participated in the interviews, organized and analyzed findings and delivered reports to Newsela's internal team.

Research across the year

Across the engagement, I contributed to multiple distinct research streams, each targeting a different area of the product and teacher experience:

  • Curriculum alignment: Evaluated how well Newsela's content matched teacher and district requirements, and the cognitive effort required to assess relevance for classroom use.
  • Platform discoverability: Understanding how teachers navigated and searched for content within the platform, and where they got lost or gave up.
  • UI for learning: Usability of the reading and markup tools, including how teachers and students used interactive features (digital annotations, quizzes, etc.), as a learning and feedback mechanism.
  • Reading literacy formats: How different content mediums such as audio, text, visual, and interactive, supported comprehension and engagement across different student levels.
  • Post-pandemic classroom dynamics: Generative research exploring how teachers were adapting instruction to uneven learning gaps following the return to in-person school.

Methods

Approach

  • Generative user interviews (45-60 minutes) via a user testing platform
  • Evaluative interviews for product and content validation
  • Qualitative analysis and synthesis reporting

Participants

K-12 teachers using Newsela's leveled-reading tools, representing varied grade levels, subject areas, and experience levels. Research took place during the post-pandemic transition back to in-person learning, a context that significantly shaped what teachers needed from digital tools.

The challenge

Newsela needed to understand how teachers were actually using the platform, not just which features they clicked, but how the product fit (or did not fit) into real classroom workflows. Each research stream was tied to a specific product question: Was content aligned to what districts required? Were annotation tools being used as intended? Could teachers find what they needed without friction?

Layered on top of these product questions was a broader contextual shift. Teachers described a classroom transformed by the pandemic: students returning with uneven knowledge, fragmented focus, and varying levels of digital fluency. Teaching had become less about delivering information and more about meeting each learner where they were.

Understanding that context was essential not just for improving individual features, but for ensuring the product did not amplify the complexity teachers were already navigating.

Key insights

Curriculum alignment was a high-stakes filter

Teachers evaluated every piece of content through the lens of their curriculum requirements. When alignment was unclear, they spent significant time assessing suitability, competing with actual lesson planning. Research surfaced this as a friction point with a direct impact on how (and whether) teachers adopted the content library.

Discoverability shaped perception of value

Teachers who could not quickly find relevant content often concluded the platform did not have what they needed, even when it did. Navigation patterns revealed that the platform's content model was not matching teachers' mental models for how they organized their curriculum. This reframed article discoverability from a UI problem to a content architecture problem.

Annotations were underused but highly valued when discovered

Usability sessions on the annotation tools revealed a gap between designed intent and actual use. Teachers who had found the annotation features described them as valuable for guiding student reading, but many had not encountered them at all, or were not sure how students experienced them on their end. The research highlighted a need for better onboarding and clearer feedback loops between teacher and student views.

Format variety mattered more than content quality alone

Across literacy format studies, teachers consistently emphasized that effectiveness depended on matching the medium to the student. Audio supported struggling readers; visual timelines aided comprehension for complex topics; text remained preferred for formal assessments. No single format worked for the whole class, reinforcing the need for flexible, multi-format content delivery.

Post-pandemic classrooms demanded differentiation at scale

Teachers were no longer teaching a single classroom rhythm, they were managing multiple learning stages simultaneously. Digital tools that assumed a uniform user were adding to their load, not reducing it. This insight ran as a common thread across every research stream, consistently pointing back to the same need: design for the range, not the average.

Research impact

Each research stream produced synthesis reports and insight presentations delivered to Newsela's product and design teams. Across the engagement, findings contributed to:

  • Curriculum alignment discussions: Research revealed that clarity around curriculum alignment is a key prioritization factor. This includes how easily alignment can be discovered and how quickly educators can evaluate whether an activity fits their lesson, highlighting lesson discovery and assessment as a measurable friction point in content adoption.
  • Content architecture conversations: Discoverability findings reflected the need for better surfacing the navigation gaps in finding article features (search, filtering, etc.).
  • Article features: Usability insights identified an awareness and onboarding gap, informing discussions about how to better surface and explain interactive tools (timelines, quizzes, annotations among others).
  • Multi-format content approach: Literacy format research reinforced the value of format variety, supporting the case for flexible, differentiated content, and reading levels variety of the same content.

Note: Due to NDA constraints, specific product decisions and shipped changes cannot be shared. The role of research in this engagement was to inform and sharpen those decisions, not to own them.

Reflections: The parallels between learning and UX

Spending a year in conversation with teachers reshaped how I think about users. Teachers are not just users of a platform, they are experts in how people learn, how attention works, and how context shapes behavior. Listening to them closely was its own kind of research education.

What struck me most was how closely the challenges they described mapped onto the challenges of designing digital products. Both involve meeting people where they are, building confidence through structure, and understanding that individuals don't start from the same place and has its own learning processes and needs.

Similarities

Engagement and curiosity

Engagement is a key factor in how we learn. The more an interface succeeds in inviting users to explore, the more it turns browsing into genuine understanding.

Scaffolding learning

Effective interfaces are supporting structures. They break down complex tasks like a great teacher using guided flows that simplify processes, timely hints that act as just-in-time lessons, and progress tracking that builds confidence with each completed step.

Meet people where they are

Just as a skilled teacher adapts lessons to a student's prior knowledge, effective interfaces respond to user skill levels. They provide shortcuts for experts and guidance for newcomers, acknowledging that every user brings their own assumptions, skills, and needs.

The ladder to learning

Issues like discoverability, engagement, and digital literacy are not just usability problems, they are lessons to learn. Like a skilled teacher, thoughtful design can scaffold learning, spark curiosity, smooth the path, and ultimately build confidence.
