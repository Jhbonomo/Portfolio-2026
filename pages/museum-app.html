The Project

Conceptual project of an Educational mobile app for the National Museum of Rio de Janeiro, highlighting community and education programs that stayed active despite the museum's closure after a devastating fire.

For my Google UX certification, I wanted to work on something real that I would be able to gather public information to build my case, therefore I chose to design an app for the National Museum of Rio de Janeiro, following the devastating fire that damaged its collection. Beyond designing, this project taught me how to adapt and make new meanings in times of crisis.

Research Context

Without direct access to stakeholders, I turned to desk research as my primary method, combing through social media, YouTube channels, webinars, press articles, academic papers, and the museum's own digital publications to build a foundation for the project.

What emerged from that research was unexpected. Despite the closure, the museum's educational community was remarkably active online. Webinars, outreach initiatives, and ongoing publications made it clear that people were deeply invested in keeping the museum's intangible value alive, its knowledge, its networks, and its role in public education well beyond the physical space and the burned collections.

Research Findings

Benchmark research showed that most museum apps are built around the physical visit, guiding users through exhibitions and collections in-person. That model didn't apply here. The museum was closed and its collection partially destroyed. But its educational section hadn't stopped. It was the most active part of the institution online: running programs, publishing content, and maintaining the museum's presence in schools and the broader community. That gap between what museum apps typically do and what this museum actually needed became the core design opportunity.

Project opportunities and design approach

The app can expand the museum's educational reach beyond artifacts by offering teachers virtual tours, customizable lessons, and other resources. This turns classrooms and homes into extensions of the museum, making it a digital learning hub accessible anytime, anywhere.

The app was designed to serve all four. Anyone can browse the museum's collections (botanic, zoological, archaeological...), but the deeper experience is structured around education: lessons tied to those collections, activities, and a calendar keeping the community informed about ongoing museum programs.

Key Design Decisions

  • Collections as learning units: Each museum collection (botanic, zoological, etc.) is paired with educational text and exercises, so it functions as both an archive and a lesson resource
  • Classroom management for teachers: Teachers invite students into their class within the app, assign specific collections or lessons, and track completion and assessment results
  • Content creation for museum staff: The museum's education section can create and publish lesson plans directly linked to collections, establishing the content network between institution and schools
  • Activity calendar: A shared calendar of museum events and programs to keep the broader community connected to the museum's ongoing presence, even without a physical space to visit

User journeys and Wireframing

The starting point was mapping what the museum was already doing digitally: a static website with article-based content and described collections and translating that into dynamic user flows. The primary journey followed a teacher from discovering a relevant museum collection to assigning it as a lesson to their students. A parallel flow mapped how museum education staff would create and publish that content in the first place. These two flows shaped the information architecture and the relationship between the collections, lessons, and classroom layers of the app.

Prototype

The prototype demonstrated the full student-to-teacher cycle: a student opens the app and can freely browse collections, but if enrolled in a class, they see assigned lessons tied to specific collections. They complete the lesson, answer exercises, and the teacher receives that assessment data. On the teacher side, the flow starts by creating a class, inviting students, and selecting which museum collection or lesson to assign, giving teachers a structured way to bring museum content into the classroom without needing to visit the physical museum.

Reflection & Learnings

When I started this project, I assumed the design challenge would be about the museum's collections and how to digitally preserve and present what survived. But the research forced a pivot. With the physical space closed and part of the collection gone, the most alive thing about the museum was its community. That shift from designing around artifacts to designing around people and their ongoing practices was the defining moment of the project.

Having indirect contact with one stakeholder from the museum's education section helped ground the project's requirements. But given more time, the first thing I would validate is access among public school teachers. They're the primary users, and many of their students face real barriers to digital literacy and device access, constraints that would meaningfully shape the app's complexity, onboarding, and offline considerations.

Key Learnings

  • The pivot is the research: The most important design decision wasn't a UI choice, it was realizing the museum's value lived in its educational community and not its physical space. That reframing came entirely from desk research.
  • Secondary research as a design tool: Without direct user access, public information such as webinars, YouTube channels, social media, and other publications can reveal genuine needs and active communities that shape a project's direction
  • Serving what already exists: The educational programs weren't invented for this app, they were already happening. The design job was to support and extend them, not create something new from scratch
  • Context shapes constraints: Designing for public school teachers means accounting for unequal access to technology among students, a real constraint that would need to be front-loaded in any future iteration
